The mission

3:00 PM. Ten minutes on the agenda.

You have the evidence (Session 1), the changes (Inning 1), the knowledge hub (Inning 2), a readiness assessment (Inning 3) and validated numbers (Inning 4). Elena and the President of Business Operations have ten minutes and will make the call in the room.

Build a one-page decision brief in Word and a three-slide storyline in PowerPoint. Then let Copilot play the toughest person in the room.

☝ Call your shotPredict before you play. Wrong guesses teach the most.

Which slide headline is strongest?

A headline should be a conclusion with a number and a consequence. Read only your slide titles in order: they should tell the whole story.

Game plan

  • Turn research findings into a professional decision brief.
  • Organize information into an executive storyline.
  • Create and refine presentation content with Copilot.
  • Summarize evidence, options, risks and next steps.
  • Use Copilot to challenge assumptions and anticipate questions.
  • Review every AI-generated number and claim before sharing.

The storyline: three slides

Executives don't want the investigation. They want the answer, the why, and the ask. Lead with the recommendation, and let the evidence follow.

This is one strong answer, not the only one. Your recommendation should follow your Inning 4 scenario.

Play-by-play

  1. Draft the decision brief in Word

    Open a blank document in Word (Create blank document), rename it in the title bar, and open the Copilot pane. Attach the three source files before you paste the prompt: select + (Add sources, skills, and more) > Add content, search for each file name and pick it. Each file appears as a chip above the prompt box. Keep Allow editing selected so Copilot writes into the document. In our test, Copilot in Word opened and used the Excel budget model; if yours can't, paste your scenario table from Inning 4 into the prompt instead.

    Copilot in Word
    Draft a one-page executive decision brief titled "Riverfront Rally Weekend - Revised Promotion Plan (June 17, 2027)".
Use /09_Task_Force_Findings_Session1, /06_Plot_Twist_Memo_June17 and /Rally_Weekend_Budget_Model.
Structure:
1. Recommendation (2 sentences, first)
2. What we discovered (3-4 bullets with evidence)
3. What the numbers mean (a small table: scenario | net contribution | vs $2.4M objective)
4. Options considered (accept BRE as signed / counter / decline; air-freight vs make-good), with pros, cons and dollar impact
5. Risks and how we mitigate them
6. Decisions needed today, with an owner and time for each
Label forecasts as estimates. Do not include any number that is not in the source files or derived from them, and show the derivation for any number you calculate.
    📎 Attach the files first. Pasted, the /FileName text is just a label. To link a file, type / and pick it, or attach it with +.
    What you should see

    Copilot works for 2 to 3 minutes and shows its steps, then a Done / Undo bar so you can keep or undo its edits. Expect a structured brief with the recommendation up top and a derivation line under the numbers table. It may run past one page, and Copilot may say it can't confirm the length. Expect to correct some numbers and some owners. That's what step 4 is for.

    Whose recommendation is it? Copilot can only recommend from what it can see. In our test it chose the make-good plan ($2,408,000 − $4,800 = $2,403,200), because ideas from your Inning 4 dugout, such as the Father's Day bundle, aren't in any source file. If your plan differs, add one line to the prompt ("Our recommended plan is …, from our Inning 4 scenario") or paste your scenario table. The recommendation is yours; Copilot drafts it.

  2. Sharpen it

    Copilot in Word
    Rewrite the Recommendation so a busy executive can approve it in 15 seconds: one sentence of decision, one sentence of why, one sentence of cost. Then cut the whole brief by 20% without losing a number.
    What you should see

    A three-part Recommendation (decision, why, cost). Copilot may also list what it cut: read that list. In our test it cut about 10%, not 20%, and dropped the caveat that Riverbend, not us, chooses between the credit and a replacement night. Put back any caveat you need.

  3. Build the three-slide deck in PowerPoint

    In PowerPoint, create a blank presentation and use Copilot to build it from your decision brief. Save the brief to OneDrive first (Word for the web does this automatically), then attach it with + > Add content. Copilot may first ask you to Choose a design style for your deck. Pick one, then check that it goes on to build your three slides. In our test it lost the original request after the style question, and the prompt had to be sent again.

    Copilot in PowerPoint
    Create a 3-slide executive presentation from /[Your decision brief file].
Slide 1 "What we discovered": the 3 exceptions that matter, each with its evidence.
Slide 2 "What the numbers mean": net contribution by scenario vs the $2.4M objective, as a simple chart or table.
Slide 3 "What we recommend and why": the recommendation, the decisions needed today, and the owner for each.
Use action titles (the title states the conclusion, not the topic). Maximum 4 bullets per slide. Add speaker notes with the supporting detail.
    Scouting report · action titles

    "Budget" is a topic. "$8K of headroom, and it's gone if we say yes to everything" is a conclusion. Executives should get the story from the titles alone.

Bottom of the 9th

Audience challenge · 5 minutes

The Skeptic in the Corner Office

Let Copilot play the hardest executive in the room. Answer its three toughest questions, then add the best answer to your speaker notes.

Solo3 questionsUpdate your deck
Copilot Chat or Copilot in PowerPoint
Act as a skeptical President of Business Operations who has 10 minutes and has been burned by optimistic forecasts before.
Read my recommendation: [paste your Recommendation section or attach the brief].
1. Ask me the 5 hardest questions you would ask in the meeting, ranked by how likely each is to change your decision.
2. For each, tell me which assumption it attacks and what evidence would satisfy you.
3. Tell me the single weakest number in my brief and why.
Be direct. Do not soften the critique.
Questions a good skeptic will ask
  • "Your plan clears $2.4M by a few thousand dollars on a forecast. What happens if Sunday rains?" (Answer: −$94,500 at 25,000 fans. Show the gap-closing levers.)
  • "Why spend $8,100 when the make-good credit is $4,800?" (Answer: the guarantee and the relationship with our biggest sponsor, and Riverbend chooses the remedy, not us.)
  • "BRE signed. Aren't we breaking our word?" (Answer: the signed version changed the scope. Our approval rule requires Ops sign-off, and we can't staff it.)
  • "Who owns the fan email change, and has it already gone out?"

If you paste only the Recommendation, the skeptic sees only what is in it. In our test it called Saturday's 41,000 forecast "unexplained" (the Plot Twist memo attributes it to Saturday pacing ahead of plan) and picked it as the weakest number. Attach the brief or the memo so the critique reflects your sources, not gaps in what you pasted.

Umpire review

The final pre-send check
  • Every number traces to a source file or a shown derivation.
  • Forecasts say "estimate". Decisions say "recommended", not "approved".
  • No AI-generated images or charts misstate the data (check axis starts and labels).
  • Speaker notes don't contain anything you wouldn't want forwarded.

🎙 Postgame press conferenceBig Idea #10 of 11
The big idea

Lead with the answer

Executives buy conclusions they can trust: the headline first, every number sourced, every risk owned, and the ask explicit.

1

The answer goes first

The recommendation, then the three reasons, then the evidence. If they stop reading after the first line, they still have the answer.

2

Every number has a home

Every figure on a slide traces to a file, a row or the simulator. AI-written decks are confident and fluent, so check each figure before you share.

3

Rehearse against the skeptic

Use Copilot as a sparring partner to find the question you can't answer yet, before the CFO asks it.

Dugout notes

Dugout notes · instructor answer key

Timing (20 min): 2 min storyline · 5 min Word brief · 5 min PowerPoint · 3 min review table · 5 min skeptic challenge.

Typical Copilot errors to watch for: quoting the BRE fee as profit (ignoring the $53,500 hat cost); stating "15,000 bobbleheads ready" (from the plan, not the findings); counting fixed cost once; calling the bucket hats "approved" because the memo says "signed"; labeling the all-conservative weekend (−$293,800 with the make-good) as "a storm Sunday" when Sunday alone is −$94,500; naming Ryan Castellano (BRE's contact) as an internal owner; a slide title that says "only" one plan clears $2.4M while its own chart shows other scenarios above the line.

Facilitation tip: have one volunteer read their slide titles aloud, titles only. If the room gets the story, the deck works.

If Copilot in PowerPoint isn't available: ask Copilot Chat for the 3-slide outline with titles, bullets and speaker notes, then paste it into PowerPoint manually. The storytelling lesson is the same.
